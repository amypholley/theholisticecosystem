Vitamins, Minerals, and Micronutrients

Vitamins are organic compounds the body needs in small amounts to support normal function. Minerals are inorganic nutrients that help build structures, regulate fluid balance, support enzymes, and keep body systems working. Together, vitamins and minerals are often called micronutrients because the body needs smaller amounts of them than macronutrients like protein, carbohydrates, and fats.

Small does not mean unimportant. Micronutrients help with energy production, immune function, brain health, mood regulation, sleep, hormone production, muscle function, heart health, bone health, recovery, and healing. A varied eating pattern can provide many of these nutrients, while some people may need extra attention because of medical conditions, medications, pregnancy, age, dietary patterns, or absorption challenges.

This page is educational. It can help you learn what nutrients do and where they are found, but it is not a substitute for medical care, lab testing, or individualized nutrition guidance.

Supplements

A food-first approach is usually the safest starting point because whole foods provide fiber, protein, fats, carbohydrates, antioxidants, and many nutrients together. Supplements may be appropriate when intake is low, lab testing shows deficiency, pregnancy increases needs, absorption is limited, or a clinician recommends them.

When They May Help

Supplements can help fill specific gaps, such as B12 for vegan diets or vitamin D when levels are low.

Interactions

Supplements can interact with medications, surgery, medical conditions, and other supplements.

Too Much Can Harm

Excess iron, vitamin A, vitamin D, selenium, iodine, or B6 can cause problems.

Quality Matters

Third-party testing can help verify purity, dose accuracy, and contamination screening.

Professional Guidance

Ask a qualified healthcare professional before using supplements for deficiency, symptoms, medications, or lab concerns.

Laboratory Testing

Lab testing can help identify deficiencies, excesses, anemia, inflammation patterns, kidney/liver concerns, or thyroid issues. A healthcare professional can decide which tests fit the situation.

Vitamin D

Evaluates vitamin D status and may be ordered for bone health, low sun exposure, or deficiency risk.

Vitamin B12 & Folate

May be checked with anemia, neurological symptoms, vegan diets, pregnancy, or absorption concerns.

Ferritin & Iron Studies

Evaluate iron stores and iron transport. Often discussed with fatigue, heavy bleeding, restless legs, or anemia.

CBC

A complete blood count screens red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and anemia patterns.

CMP

A comprehensive metabolic panel looks at electrolytes, kidney markers, liver markers, glucose, and proteins.

Magnesium

Blood magnesium may be ordered with cramps, rhythm concerns, medication use, or electrolyte issues.

Thyroid Testing

Thyroid labs may be discussed when fatigue, temperature sensitivity, weight changes, mood changes, or iodine-related concerns are present.

Special Populations

Children & Teenagers

Growth increases needs for iron, calcium, vitamin D, zinc, and overall nutrient variety.

Pregnant Individuals

Folate, iron, iodine, choline, vitamin D, and B12 may need closer attention.

Older Adults

B12, vitamin D, protein, calcium, hydration, and medication interactions may become more important.

Vegetarians & Vegans

B12, iron, zinc, iodine, calcium, vitamin D, and omega-3 intake may need planning.

Athletes

Iron, electrolytes, magnesium, vitamin D, calcium, and overall energy intake may affect performance and recovery.

GI Disorders

Conditions affecting digestion or absorption can increase deficiency risk and may require medical monitoring.

Long-Term Medications

Some medications affect B12, magnesium, vitamin D, folate, potassium, sodium, or other nutrients.

Final Takeaways

  • Variety matters because different foods provide different nutrients.
  • Whole foods provide more than isolated vitamins and minerals.
  • Deficiencies can affect physical and mental wellness.
  • Supplements are not substitutes for a healthy overall diet.
  • Individual needs vary based on health, life stage, medications, and access to food.
