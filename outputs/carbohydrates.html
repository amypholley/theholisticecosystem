Carbohydrates

What Are Carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are the body's primary source of energy. They help fuel the brain, muscles, organs, and daily activities. Carbohydrates are found in fruits, vegetables, grains, legumes, dairy products, and many other foods.

Carbohydrates provide more than energy. Many carbohydrate-containing foods also supply fiber, vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other important nutrients.

Daily Carbohydrate Guidelines

General Recommendation: Approximately 45-65% of total daily calories from carbohydrates.

Fiber: Fiber is an important type of carbohydrate that supports digestive and overall health. See the Fiber page for detailed recommendations and food sources.

Learn More About Fiber

Individual needs vary based on age, activity level, health conditions, goals, and medical guidance.

Types of Carbohydrates

Simple Carbohydrates

Simple carbohydrates are digested more quickly and can provide rapid energy.

Examples: Fruit, milk, honey, table sugar.

Complex Carbohydrates

Complex carbohydrates generally contain more fiber and nutrients and are digested more gradually.

Examples: Oats, beans, brown rice, sweet potatoes.

Natural Sugars

Naturally occurring sugars found in foods often come packaged with fiber, water, vitamins, and minerals.

Examples: Fruit, vegetables, dairy products.

Added Sugars

Sugars added during processing, cooking, or preparation.

Examples: Soft drinks, candy, desserts, sweetened beverages.

Fiber

Fiber is a type of carbohydrate that the body does not fully digest. Unlike many other carbohydrates, fiber passes through the digestive system largely intact and plays an important role in overall health.

Fiber is generally categorized into:

Soluble Fiber

  • Dissolves in water
  • Found in foods such as oats, beans, apples, and citrus fruits

Insoluble Fiber

  • Adds bulk to stool
  • Found in foods such as whole grains, vegetables, nuts, and seeds

Many foods contain a combination of both types of fiber.

Fiber can support:

  • Digestive health
  • Fullness and satiety
  • Heart health
  • Blood sugar management

Whole Grains vs Refined Grains

Whole Grains

Contain the bran, germ, and endosperm.

Examples: Brown rice, oats, quinoa, whole wheat bread.

Benefits: Typically contain more fiber and micronutrients.

Refined Grains

Have some portions removed during processing.

Examples: White rice, white bread, traditional pasta.

Important Note: Refined grains are not inherently "bad" foods. They can still be part of a balanced eating pattern. Whole grains simply tend to provide more fiber and nutrients.

No single food determines health. Foods exist on a spectrum and can fit into an overall balanced diet.
