Vitamins, Minerals, and Micronutrients

Vitamins are organic compounds the body needs in small amounts to support normal function. Minerals are inorganic nutrients that help build structures, regulate fluid balance, support enzymes, and keep body systems working. Together, vitamins and minerals are often called micronutrients because the body needs smaller amounts of them than macronutrients like protein, carbohydrates, and fats.

Small does not mean unimportant. Micronutrients help with energy production, immune function, brain health, mood regulation, sleep, hormone production, muscle function, heart health, bone health, recovery, and healing. A varied eating pattern can provide many of these nutrients, while some people may need extra attention because of medical conditions, medications, pregnancy, age, dietary patterns, or absorption challenges.

This page is educational. It can help you learn what nutrients do and where they are found, but it is not a substitute for medical care, lab testing, or individualized nutrition guidance.

Fat-Soluble Vitamins

Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with dietary fat and can be stored in the body. Because they can accumulate, excess intake from supplements may carry more risk than food-based intake.

Vitamin A

Supports vision, immune defense, skin, growth, and cell development.

  • Deficiency: night vision changes, dry eyes, immune concerns.
  • Excess: high supplement doses can affect the liver and pregnancy safety.
  • Sources: liver, eggs, dairy, sweet potatoes, carrots, spinach.
  • Fact: plant beta-carotene can be converted into vitamin A.

Vitamin D

Supports calcium absorption, bones, immune function, muscle function, and may relate to mood.

  • Deficiency: low bone mineralization, muscle aches, fatigue-like symptoms.
  • Excess: too much supplemental vitamin D can raise calcium levels.
  • Sources: sunlight exposure, fatty fish, fortified milk, fortified foods.
  • Fact: blood testing is commonly used to evaluate vitamin D status.

Vitamin E

An antioxidant that helps protect cells and supports immune function.

  • Deficiency: uncommon, but may occur with fat absorption disorders.
  • Excess: high-dose supplements may interact with blood-thinning medications.
  • Sources: nuts, seeds, wheat germ oil, sunflower seeds, almonds.
  • Fact: vitamin E is actually a family of related compounds.

Vitamin K

Supports normal blood clotting and bone metabolism.

  • Deficiency: unusual bleeding risk, low intake, or absorption issues.
  • Excess: food intake is usually not a concern, but medication interactions matter.
  • Sources: leafy greens, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, some fermented foods.
  • Fact: people on warfarin should discuss vitamin K consistency with clinicians.

Water-Soluble Vitamins

Water-soluble vitamins dissolve in water and are usually not stored in large amounts. Regular intake matters, and some can still cause problems at high supplemental doses.

Vitamin C

Supports collagen, wound healing, iron absorption, antioxidant protection, and immune function.

  • Low intake: gum issues, poor wound healing, fatigue-like symptoms.
  • High intake: may cause digestive upset.
  • Sources: citrus, strawberries, bell peppers, potatoes, broccoli.
  • Fact: vitamin C improves absorption of non-heme iron from plants.

Vitamin B1 (Thiamine)

Helps convert carbohydrates into energy and supports nerve function.

  • Low intake: weakness, nerve symptoms, confusion in severe cases.
  • Sources: pork, whole grains, legumes, fortified grains.
  • Fact: alcohol use can increase risk of thiamine deficiency.

Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin)

Supports energy metabolism, skin, eyes, and antioxidant systems.

  • Low intake: mouth cracks, sore throat, skin changes.
  • Sources: dairy, eggs, almonds, mushrooms, fortified grains.
  • Fact: riboflavin gives some supplements a bright yellow color.

Vitamin B3 (Niacin)

Supports energy metabolism, skin, nerves, and digestive function.

  • Low intake: severe deficiency can affect skin, digestion, and cognition.
  • High intake: high-dose supplements can cause flushing and liver concerns.
  • Sources: poultry, fish, meat, peanuts, fortified grains.

Vitamin B5 (Pantothenic Acid)

Helps make coenzyme A, which supports energy production and fatty acid metabolism.

  • Low intake: rare; may include fatigue-like symptoms.
  • Sources: chicken, eggs, mushrooms, avocado, whole grains.
  • Fact: found in many foods, so deficiency is uncommon.

Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine)

Supports amino acid metabolism, neurotransmitters, red blood cells, and immune function.

  • Low intake: mood changes, anemia, skin changes, nerve symptoms.
  • High intake: excessive supplemental B6 can damage nerves.
  • Sources: poultry, fish, potatoes, bananas, chickpeas.

Vitamin B7 (Biotin)

Supports metabolism of fats, carbohydrates, and proteins.

  • Low intake: rare; may affect hair, skin, or nerves.
  • Sources: eggs, salmon, sunflower seeds, sweet potatoes.
  • Fact: biotin supplements can interfere with some lab tests.

Vitamin B9 (Folate)

Supports DNA synthesis, red blood cells, pregnancy needs, and methylation pathways.

  • Low intake: anemia, fatigue-like symptoms, pregnancy risks.
  • Sources: leafy greens, beans, lentils, citrus, fortified grains.
  • Fact: folic acid is the synthetic form used in fortification and many supplements.

Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin)

Supports nerves, red blood cells, DNA synthesis, and brain function.

  • Low intake: anemia, numbness, tingling, memory or mood symptoms.
  • Sources: fish, meat, dairy, eggs, fortified foods.
  • Fact: vegans and some older adults may need special attention to B12.

Major Minerals

Calcium

Supports bones, teeth, muscle contraction, nerves, and heart rhythm.

  • Low intake: bone health concerns over time.
  • Excess: high supplemental intake may raise kidney stone risk.
  • Sources: dairy, fortified foods, tofu, sardines, leafy greens.

Phosphorus

Supports bones, teeth, energy molecules, and cell membranes.

  • Low intake: uncommon.
  • Excess: kidney disease can change phosphorus needs.
  • Sources: dairy, meat, poultry, fish, beans, nuts.

Magnesium

Supports muscle and nerve function, blood pressure, glucose metabolism, stress response, and energy production.

  • Low intake: cramps, weakness, fatigue-like symptoms, irregular rhythm concerns.
  • Excess: high supplemental doses may cause diarrhea or medication interactions.
  • Sources: nuts, seeds, beans, whole grains, leafy greens.

Sodium

Supports fluid balance, nerves, and muscle contraction.

  • Low levels: can occur with illness, medications, or fluid imbalance.
  • Excess: high intake may affect blood pressure in some people.
  • Sources: salt, packaged foods, soups, sauces.

Potassium

Supports fluid balance, blood pressure, muscles, nerves, and heart rhythm.

  • Low levels: weakness, cramps, rhythm concerns.
  • Excess: kidney disease and medications can raise risk.
  • Sources: potatoes, beans, bananas, dairy, leafy greens.

Chloride

Supports fluid balance and stomach acid production.

  • Low levels: uncommon outside medical situations.
  • Sources: salt, seaweed, tomatoes, celery, processed foods.

Sulfur

Part of sulfur-containing amino acids and compounds used in tissues, enzymes, and detoxification pathways.

  • Essential status: obtained through protein foods rather than a separate daily target.
  • Sources: eggs, meat, fish, legumes, onions, garlic, cruciferous vegetables.

Trace Minerals

Iron

Needed for oxygen transport, energy, immune function, and brain health.

  • Low levels: fatigue, weakness, shortness of breath, restless legs.
  • Excess: can be harmful; supplementation should be guided.
  • Sources: red meat, poultry, seafood, beans, lentils, fortified grains.

Zinc

Supports immunity, wound healing, taste, smell, reproduction, and cognition.

  • Low intake: immune concerns, poor wound healing, taste changes.
  • Excess: can interfere with copper.
  • Sources: oysters, meat, pumpkin seeds, beans, dairy.

Copper

Supports iron metabolism, connective tissue, nerves, and antioxidant systems.

  • Low intake: anemia-like symptoms, neurological concerns.
  • Sources: shellfish, nuts, seeds, cocoa, organ meats.

Manganese

Supports bone formation, metabolism, and antioxidant enzymes.

  • Low intake: uncommon.
  • Sources: whole grains, nuts, legumes, tea.

Selenium

Supports thyroid metabolism, antioxidant enzymes, and immune function.

  • Low intake: thyroid and immune concerns in some contexts.
  • Excess: too much can cause hair, nail, and nerve symptoms.
  • Sources: Brazil nuts, seafood, meats, eggs.

Iodine

Needed to make thyroid hormones that support metabolism and brain development.

  • Low intake: thyroid enlargement, fatigue-like symptoms, pregnancy concerns.
  • Excess: can also affect thyroid function.
  • Sources: iodized salt, seaweed, dairy, seafood.

Chromium

May support insulin action and carbohydrate metabolism.

  • Essential status: recognized as a trace nutrient, but needs are small.
  • Sources: whole grains, meats, broccoli, grape juice.

Molybdenum

Part of enzymes that process sulfur-containing compounds and other metabolites.

  • Low intake: very rare.
  • Sources: legumes, grains, nuts.

Fluoride

Supports tooth enamel and dental health.

  • Low intake: may relate to dental cavity risk.
  • Excess: high intake during tooth development can affect enamel appearance.
  • Sources: fluoridated water, tea, some seafood.

Emerging and Lesser-Known Minerals

Some minerals are being studied for possible roles in health, but not all are considered essential nutrients with established daily requirements.

Boron

Research explores bone, vitamin D, and hormone-related pathways. Not currently classified as essential. Sources include prunes, raisins, nuts, legumes, and avocados.

Silicon

May play a role in connective tissue and bone health. Essential status is not clearly established. Sources include whole grains, bananas, and some mineral waters.

Vanadium

Studied for glucose-related pathways, but not considered essential for humans. Sources include mushrooms, shellfish, black pepper, and grains.

Nickel

May have biological roles in tiny amounts, but essentiality for humans is not established. Sources include nuts, legumes, chocolate, and grains.

Cobalt

Cobalt is part of vitamin B12. The nutritional focus is usually adequate B12, especially for vegans and those with absorption issues.

Lithium

Nutritional lithium research is separate from prescription lithium medication. It is not considered an essential nutrient. Food and water contain trace amounts.

Vitamins, Minerals, and Mental Health

Nutrition can influence mental and neurological wellness, but nutrients are only one part of a much larger picture that includes sleep, stress, trauma, environment, medication, medical conditions, support, and access to care. Deficiencies may contribute to symptoms, but supplements do not replace mental health treatment.

Vitamin D and Depression

Low vitamin D status is associated with depression in some studies, but supplementation is not a guaranteed treatment. Testing can help clarify status.

Iron and Fatigue

Low iron or ferritin can contribute to fatigue, weakness, brain fog, restless legs, and exercise intolerance.

Vitamin B12 and Nerves

B12 deficiency can cause anemia, numbness, tingling, balance changes, memory issues, and mood symptoms.

Folate and Mood

Folate supports methylation and neurotransmitter pathways. Low folate may affect mood and energy in some people.

Magnesium and Stress

Magnesium supports muscle relaxation, nerves, sleep-related pathways, and stress physiology.

Zinc and Cognition

Zinc supports brain function, immunity, and wound healing. Both low and excessive intake can create problems.

Iodine and Brain Function

Iodine supports thyroid hormone production, which is important for metabolism, energy, and brain development.

Omega-3 Reference

Omega-3 fats are not vitamins or minerals, but they are often discussed with brain health because they support cell membranes and inflammatory balance.

Common Deficiencies

Vitamin D Deficiency

Risk factors: limited sun exposure, darker skin, winter climates, fat absorption issues. Possible signs: bone pain, muscle aches, low energy. Speak with a healthcare professional about testing.

Magnesium Deficiency

Risk factors: low intake, gastrointestinal disorders, some medications. Possible signs: cramps, weakness, rhythm concerns, fatigue-like symptoms.

Iron Deficiency

Risk factors: heavy menstrual bleeding, pregnancy, low intake, GI blood loss, endurance training. Possible signs: fatigue, shortness of breath, dizziness, restless legs.

Vitamin B12 Deficiency

Risk factors: vegan diets, older age, stomach surgery, low stomach acid, some medications. Possible signs: numbness, tingling, anemia, memory or mood changes.

Folate Deficiency

Risk factors: low intake, pregnancy, alcohol use, some medications. Possible signs: anemia, low energy, mouth soreness.

Potassium Deficiency

Risk factors: vomiting, diarrhea, diuretics, low intake. Possible signs: muscle weakness, cramps, constipation, rhythm concerns.

Calcium Deficiency

Risk factors: low intake, vitamin D deficiency, restrictive diets, absorption issues. Possible signs: bone health concerns over time, cramps, tingling.

Supplements

A food-first approach is usually the safest starting point because whole foods provide fiber, protein, fats, carbohydrates, antioxidants, and many nutrients together. Supplements may be appropriate when intake is low, lab testing shows deficiency, pregnancy increases needs, absorption is limited, or a clinician recommends them.

When They May Help

Supplements can help fill specific gaps, such as B12 for vegan diets or vitamin D when levels are low.

Interactions

Supplements can interact with medications, surgery, medical conditions, and other supplements.

Too Much Can Harm

Excess iron, vitamin A, vitamin D, selenium, iodine, or B6 can cause problems.

Quality Matters

Third-party testing can help verify purity, dose accuracy, and contamination screening.

Professional Guidance

Ask a qualified healthcare professional before using supplements for deficiency, symptoms, medications, or lab concerns.

Laboratory Testing

Lab testing can help identify deficiencies, excesses, anemia, inflammation patterns, kidney/liver concerns, or thyroid issues. A healthcare professional can decide which tests fit the situation.

Vitamin D

Evaluates vitamin D status and may be ordered for bone health, low sun exposure, or deficiency risk.

Vitamin B12 & Folate

May be checked with anemia, neurological symptoms, vegan diets, pregnancy, or absorption concerns.

Ferritin & Iron Studies

Evaluate iron stores and iron transport. Often discussed with fatigue, heavy bleeding, restless legs, or anemia.

CBC

A complete blood count screens red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and anemia patterns.

CMP

A comprehensive metabolic panel looks at electrolytes, kidney markers, liver markers, glucose, and proteins.

Magnesium

Blood magnesium may be ordered with cramps, rhythm concerns, medication use, or electrolyte issues.

Thyroid Testing

Thyroid labs may be discussed when fatigue, temperature sensitivity, weight changes, mood changes, or iodine-related concerns are present.

Special Populations

Children & Teenagers

Growth increases needs for iron, calcium, vitamin D, zinc, and overall nutrient variety.

Pregnant Individuals

Folate, iron, iodine, choline, vitamin D, and B12 may need closer attention.

Older Adults

B12, vitamin D, protein, calcium, hydration, and medication interactions may become more important.

Vegetarians & Vegans

B12, iron, zinc, iodine, calcium, vitamin D, and omega-3 intake may need planning.

Athletes

Iron, electrolytes, magnesium, vitamin D, calcium, and overall energy intake may affect performance and recovery.

GI Disorders

Conditions affecting digestion or absorption can increase deficiency risk and may require medical monitoring.

Long-Term Medications

Some medications affect B12, magnesium, vitamin D, folate, potassium, sodium, or other nutrients.

Final Takeaways

  • Variety matters because different foods provide different nutrients.
  • Whole foods provide more than isolated vitamins and minerals.
  • Deficiencies can affect physical and mental wellness.
  • Supplements are not substitutes for a healthy overall diet.
  • Individual needs vary based on health, life stage, medications, and access to food.
